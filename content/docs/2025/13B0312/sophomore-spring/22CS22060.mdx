    en: 'This course centers on the integrated thinking of "game theory + optimization" and aims to help students master the basic concepts of game theory (such as players, strategies, payoffs, Nash equilibrium, etc.), the characteristics and equilibrium analysis logic of different types of games (zero-sum and non-zero-sum games, complete and incomplete information games, simultaneous and sequential games, coalition games, auction games, etc.), while proficiently using core optimization tools such as linear programming and Lagrange multipliers. It enables students to understand and realize the transformation between game problems and optimization models, and ultimately possess the ability to model and analyze decision optimization problems in fields such as economics, management science, and resource allocation. This is specifically achieved through three main course objectives: first, mastering the basics of game theory and optimization tools, and being able to transform classic static games into standardized optimization models; second, being familiar with the equilibrium characteristics and solution logic of different games, and being able to derive and verify equilibrium results using backward induction, max-min strategies combined with optimization tools; third, mastering integrated modeling methods, and being able to construct comprehensive "strategy analysis + goal optimization" models for economic competition and market decision-making. These three objectives correspond respectively to the graduation requirements of "knowledge reserve" (mathematical and information science foundation), "problem analysis" (knowledge application and innovation ability), and "use of modern tools" (numerical software use and algorithm development ability). The course content system is clear, covering 24 teaching modules. The first 16 modules are classroom theoretical teaching (2 class hours per module), including an introduction to games and classification, simultaneous games and payoff matrix analysis, optimization tools and their connection with games, the definition and application of Nash equilibrium (pure strategies and mixed strategies), the reaction function method and multiple Nash equilibria, sequential games and backward induction, first-mover/second-mover advantages, tree-form games and perfect games, repeated games, commitments, threats and credibility, zero-sum games, optimization modeling and solution of game equilibria (part 1 and part 2), bargaining problems (including the K-S solution), coalition games and simultaneous games with incomplete information, auction mechanisms and sequential games with incomplete information, all adopting "classroom teaching + case-based" teaching methods. The last 8 modules are experimental and practical teaching (2 class hours per module), focusing on the application of Python tools, including building game matrices based on numpy (reproducing the Prisoner''s Dilemma, Boxed Pigs Game), solving pure strategy Nash equilibria based on scipy.optimize, calculating mixed strategy Nash equilibria based on scipy and numpy (including visualization), implementing backward induction for sequential games based on recursive functions (reproducing the Stackelberg model), solving max-min strategies for zero-sum games based on pulp, simulating the Hotelling model based on numpy and matplotlib (analyzing optimal location selection), simulating auction mechanisms based on probability distributions (statistical sellers'' expected revenue), and implementing the K-S solution for bargaining based on scipy.optimize.minimize, so as to strengthen the practical transformation of theoretical knowledge.'
