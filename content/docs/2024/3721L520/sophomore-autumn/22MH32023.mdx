Especially in iatrology, these micro/nanorobots are expected to access hard-to-reach areas of the human body in a minimally invasive manner, such as the circulatory system, gastrointestinal tract, vasculature, brain, and eyes, resulting in the promising potential of micro/nanorobots for various biomedical applications, including targeted drug delivery, cancer treatment, diagnosis and monitoring, minimally invasive surgery, and tissue engineering. In the process of implementing various biomedical tasks, locomotion behavior is of great significance for micro/nanorobots. Unlike macroscale robots, when the size of robots reduces to the micro/nanoscale, their movement in the fluid is dominated by viscous forces rather than inertial forces due to the low Reynolds number environment. As a result, inertia-based propulsion strategies become ineffective in the microworld, in other words, a microscopic scallop can only execute reciprocal motion without net displacement at a low Reynolds number environment, which is concluded by Purcell’s Scallop Theorem. To overcome the limits caused by the Scallop Theorem, micro/nanorobots with flexible structures are developed by the inspiration of microorganisms with flagella or cilia. Additionally, strategies involving designing asymmetric geometric structures, providing interfaces, or utilizing asymmetric actuation sources can also be employed to break the Scallop Theorem. The energy sources used to power the micro/nanorobots are mainly divided into chemical energy sources, and external field energy sources (such as magnetic field, optical field, acoustic wave, and electric field.
